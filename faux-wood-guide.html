Buying guide

Faux wood blinds: the complete buying guide.

Slat width, routeless slats, valances, weight limits and the two rooms where faux wood beats real wood outright. Everything you need before you configure a size.

Shop at The Home DepotOrder free samples

Faux wood versus real wood

Real wood is lighter and has genuine grain variation. Faux wood is a polymer composite that will not warp in humidity and costs meaningfully less at the same slat size. In a kitchen, bathroom or laundry, composite is not a compromise; it is the correct specification.

Choosing a slat width

  • 2" slats: standard windows, a smaller stack when raised, slightly more privacy when closed.
  • 2 1/2" slats: large windows, fewer lines across the glass, a clearer view when tilted open, a more architectural look.

On any window wider than about 48", the wider slat almost always looks more expensive for very little extra money.

Routeless slats

Standard slats have holes routed through them for the lift cords, which produce a row of pinholes of light. Routeless slats move the route to the back edge, so you lose the pinholes. On a bedroom or a street-facing window it is the single best upgrade on this product.

If the room needs to be dark, order routeless. If it is a hallway, do not bother.

Weight and width limits

  • Up to 48": standard two-bracket installation.
  • 48" to 72": a centre support bracket is included and required.
  • Above 72": consider motorization, or split the opening into two blinds on one headrail.

Colour and finish

Match the trim, not the wall. Bright white beside antique white trim reads as a mistake from across the room. Order both swatches and hold them against the casing in daylight.

The valance

A matched 3" crown valance is included. Fit it. It hides the headrail and it is the difference between a finished window and a visible mechanism.

What to avoid

  • Cutting slats down after delivery. It voids the warranty and never looks right.
  • Furniture polish, which leaves a film that attracts dust.
  • Skipping the centre bracket on a wide blind, which bows the headrail within a season.
