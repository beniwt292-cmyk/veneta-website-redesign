Energy

Beat the summer heat with cellular shades

Why the honeycomb structure works, which configuration insulates best, and what to do about a west-facing wall.

June 18, 20267 min readVeneta editorial team

The window is the weak point

Walls have insulation. Windows have glass. In summer, sunlight passes through the glass and turns into heat inside the room, and that heat does not leave the same way it arrived. Anything you put at the glass to slow that exchange helps.

How a honeycomb cell works

A cellular shade traps a pocket of still air between the room and the window. Still air is a poor conductor, so heat moves through it slowly. A double cell shade traps two pockets instead of one, which is why it insulates better than a single cell of the same fabric.

The best configuration for heat

  • Double cell, for two air pockets.
  • Blackout fabric, because the foil liner also reflects radiant heat.
  • SmartPrivacy® side channels, which close the air gap at the edges of the shade.
  • A tight inside mount, so there is less bypass around the shade.

West-facing rooms need a second layer

Late afternoon sun arrives at a low angle and hits you directly. A cellular shade handles the heat, but you may not want the room fully dark at 5pm. Pair a 3% solar screen for the day with a blackout cellular shade for the evening on the same window.

Habits matter as much as hardware

Close the shades on the sunny side before the room heats up, not after. Motorization with a schedule does this reliably, which is the practical argument for it in a hot climate.

We do not publish a single energy-savings percentage, because the honest answer depends on your glass, your orientation and your climate. What we can say is that double cell blackout with side channels is the most insulating combination we build.
